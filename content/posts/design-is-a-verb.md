Most people think of design as a noun. A thing that gets made. A logo, a layout, a prototype delivered on Friday.

But the designers I admire most treat it as a verb. Something you do continuously, not something you finish.

## The process is the point

When you treat design as a noun, you optimize for the artifact. You ask: *is this good?* When you treat it as a verb, you optimize for the thinking. You ask: *what did I learn making this?*

That shift changes everything — how you receive feedback, how you handle iteration, how you explain your decisions to others.

## What this means for learning

If design is a verb, then learning design means practicing it. Not studying it from a distance, not collecting inspiration boards, not reading articles like this one.

It means making things that don't work, understanding why, and making them again.

> The best design education is a long series of small failures in quick succession.

I've found that cross-disciplinary thinkers — writers, scientists, musicians who come to design — often grasp this intuitively. They already know that craft is a practice, not a destination.

## Three things to try this week

1. Redesign something that already exists. Pick any interface you use daily and redesign one screen.
2. Write a brief for yourself. Constraints are a designer's best friend.
3. Show your work to someone outside the design world. Their confusion is data.

Design is a verb. Go do it.
